What is Puja?

Key Stage 3 Religious Education Guide

Puja is the central act of worship in Hinduism. It is a ritual performed to host, honour, and worship one or more deities. For Hindus, Puja is not just about reciting prayers; it is a highly interactive, ritualistic expression of love, devotion, and surrender designed to build a real, reciprocal spiritual connection between the human devotee and the divine.

Where Worship Takes Place

Puja can take place in a communal setting or in total privacy, reflecting the deeply adaptable nature of Hindu practice:

  • The Home Shrine (Ghar Mandir): Most Hindu households dedicate a quiet room, corner, or cupboard to a family shrine. This shrine houses pictures or statues of specific deities. Home Puja is usually a daily routine, performed in the morning or evening by family members to bless the household and ground everyday tasks in spirituality.
  • The Temple (Mandir): A Mandir is considered the earthly home of the deities. Public Pujas are led by a trained priest known as a Brahmin, who speaks holy scripts in Sanskrit. These large gatherings build strong community bonds and are highly synchronized, featuring mass prayers, loud music, and shared meals.

Engaging the 5 Senses

A core theological concept behind Hindu ritual is that the mind is easily distracted by the material world. To counteract this, a traditional Puja uses sensory objects on a worship tray to occupy all five human senses, redirecting the devotee's entire focus toward the divine:

1. Sight (Darshan) Devotees gaze intently into the eyes of the sacred image or statue, known as the Murti. Hindus believe the deity is spiritually present within the Murti. This visual exchange allows the worshipper to see God and receive divine blessings directly back through the eyes.
2. Hearing A brass bell called a Ghanti is rung repeatedly to mark the start of the ritual, awaken the mind, and ward off negative thoughts. Worshippers also chant ancient sound formulas called Mantras (like 'Om') and sing devotional hymns known as Bhajans.
3. Smell Incense sticks, or Agarbatti, are burned alongside fresh flowers like marigolds or jasmines. The sweet fragrances serve a dual purpose: they purify the air to create a sanctified space and symbolize the devotee's prayers silently rising up to heaven.
4. Touch Devotees touch the base of the Murti or bow flat on the floor to show complete humility. They also gently wave their cupped hands over the holy flame of the burning lamp, then press their warm palms to their eyes and forehead to absorb the deity's energy.
5. Taste Pure offerings of fruit, cooked rice, water, and traditional sweets are placed before the Murti. Hindus believe the deity symbolically consumes the spiritual essence of the food. The physical food left behind becomes blessed and is distributed to the congregation to eat as Prashad.

The Aarti Ceremony

The climax of a standard Puja is the Aarti ceremony. A designated tray holding a small metal lamp with burning cotton wicks (usually soaked in clarified butter, or ghee) is slowly rotated in a clockwise direction in front of the Murti. This circular movement represents the cycle of life and reminds worshippers that God is the absolute center of the universe. As the light is offered, the atmosphere fills with the ringing of large bells, the blowing of conch shells, and rhythmic congregational singing.

Why Puja Matters to Hindus

For Year 8 pupils analyzing the impact of religion, performing Puja carries deep philosophical importance:

  • Devotion (Bhakti): It is an expression of Bhakti Marga, the spiritual path of unselfish love and emotional attachment to a personal God as a route to salvation.
  • Duty (Dharma): Regular worship fulfills an individual's personal and cosmic duty, maintaining proper moral balance and order in their daily life.
  • Action (Karma): Performing selfless acts of devotion and maintaining positive intentions during Puja helps an individual accumulate good Karma, which is essential for progressing through the cycle of reincarnation.
